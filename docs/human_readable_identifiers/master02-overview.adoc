= Overview

Many openEHR knowledge artefacts - including archetypes, templates and terminology subsets - are identified by a _human-readable identifier_ (HRID): a structured, lexically meaningful string that encodes the reference model basis and domain concept of the artefact, together with its version. For example:

* `org.openehr::openEHR-EHR-OBSERVATION.blood_pressure.v2` - a namespaced (managed) archetype HRID;
* `openEHR-EHR-COMPOSITION.report.v1` - an unnamespaced (unmanaged) HRID.

An HRID is designed to be both human-readable and computable: sub-parts such as the reference-model class or the version can be extracted and reasoned about directly.

The computable type representing an HRID is the `Hrid` primitive, defined in the openEHR {openehr_base_latest_foundation_types}[Foundation Types^] specification as a `String` constrained to the grammar defined here; this document is the normative source for that grammar and its semantics. Concrete identifier types are specific applications of the scheme - for example `ARCHETYPE_ID` (openEHR {openehr_base_latest_base_types}[Base Types^]) and `ARCHETYPE_HRID` (openEHR {openehr_am_latest_aom2}[Archetype Object Model^]) both denote artefacts whose string form conforms to this grammar.

== Requirements

Knowledge artefacts such as archetypes, templates and terminology subsets are 'outside the software' and independent of specific implementation technologies: they are created, developed, disseminated and used independently of software release cycles. Reliable identification and referencing of such artefacts, within and across distributed authoring and consumption environments, is therefore a distinct problem from software identification, even though the two share many characteristics.

The key requirements addressed by the HRID scheme are to:

* identify and distinguish versions, variants and releases of 'source' artefacts within and across authoring environments;
* define rules for expressing and resolving _references_ between source artefacts, including version variants;
* define rules for identification of compiled / operational artefacts derived from source artefacts;
* define rules for evolving identifiers (including version) of artefacts over time, based on a standard artefact lifecycle;
* define rules for identification when artefacts are retired, moved or 'forked'.

The domain of experience assumed here is health, but the scheme applies to any domain that manages knowledge artefacts independently of software.

== Human-readable and Machine Identifiers

Two general approaches to identification exist. The first, used in software and ontology development, is the _human-readable identifier_ (HRID): an identifier that names an artefact and can also be used as a reference to connect related artefacts, for example along a specialisation / subsumption hierarchy. A typical software HRID is a class name such as `FastSortedList`. The second is the _machine identifier_ (more properly a 'machine-readable' or 'machine-resolvable' identifier) such as a GUID or ISO OID, with an accompanying de-referencing mechanism. The two approaches are complementary rather than equivalent.

A human-readable scheme supports specialisation hierarchies, multi-dimensional concept spaces and flexible versioning, and reflects the authors' and users' understanding of the concept space being modelled; it therefore supports many kinds of computational processing directly. One consequence is that a human-readable identifier _may change after initial assignment_ - typically only during early development, before the first release - for reasons such as a change of purpose or improved understanding of need.

A machine identifier, by contrast, is opaque, does not directly support versioning, and requires mapping to obtain a human-readable form; but, unlike a human-readable identifier, it does not normally change once assigned. This makes it suitable for tracking an artefact stably across every revision of its definition.

The openEHR scheme uses both:

* a machine identifier (assumed to be a GUID) is assigned when an artefact is created and does not change, whatever subsequent changes are made to the artefact definition; it enables authoring and repository tools to track an artefact over time;
* one or more namespaced HRIDs are _computed_ from identifying properties of the artefact (which properties depends on the artefact type);
* the most recent committed build of an artefact can be identified either by a build number that is part of its version identification, or by a hash over a canonical serialisation.

This specification defines the human-readable identifier. The machine identifier is held as a separate property of the artefact (for archetypes, the `uid` property of `ARCHETYPE_HRID`) and is otherwise outside the scope of this document.

== Managed and Unmanaged Artefacts

The highest-level distinction is between _managed_ and _unmanaged_ artefacts. A managed artefact carries a `namespace` - the publisher organisation's reverse domain name - prepended to a 'local' HRID. Reverse domain names are used to aid lexical sorting of identifiers and directory construction by tools. Examples:

* `org.openehr` - EHR archetype library at openEHR.org;
* `uk.nhs` - UK National Health Service;
* `edu.nci` - US National Cancer Institute.

An identifier without a namespace is assumed to denote an _unmanaged_ artefact, i.e. one with no recognised owner organisation. The `namespace` is optional for legacy reasons, since many existing artefacts predate its introduction; during a period of migration, managed artefacts progressively acquire a namespace (and a machine identifier). All managed artefacts should carry a namespace.
